Governance in A3T answers three questions for every call a person or an agent makes: **may it happen**, **who said so**, and **can we prove it later**. The answers come from four parts that work together.

| Part | What it does | Where you manage it |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity and roles** | Who someone is (sign-in, their org's own provider, keys for agents) and the roles they hold on the platform, in an org and in each project | [Identity and roles](../identity-and-roles/) |
| **Policy files** | The org's rules, roles and access as YAML files: versioned, checked before they apply, optionally approved by a second admin | [Policy files](../policy-files/), [Writing a rule](../writing-a-rule/) |
| **Agents and approvals** | What each agent may do (its own policy, budget, hosts, keys), which calls wait for a person, and how to stop it | [Agents and approvals](../agents-and-approvals/) |
| **The decision log** | Every decision, with who, what, why and the policy version, chained so edits show; kept per org, exported to your SIEM | [Decision log](../../console/decision-log/) |

## How a decision is made

Every call (a tool, a model, a flow start, a deploy, a change to a resource) is checked at its **choke point** against **Cedar**, as the person it is for, with their role where the call lands.

1. **Who.** A person, or an agent acting for one. An agent carries at most the roles its own policy allows (its *role ceiling*), and never more than the person it acts for.
2. **The baseline**, compiled into A3T, denies by default.
3. **The org's enforced policy files** that apply to the project, the flow or the agent add `forbid` rules on top. A `forbid` always wins.
4. **Allow, deny, or ask.** A rule can say a call needs a person's approval first: the run waits, the person answers in the console, and the run carries on (or stops).
5. **Logged.** The decision goes to the decision log with the ids and versions of the policies that made it.

<Aside type="tip">
A refusal names the policy that refused it, for example `Refused by policy acme/no-deletes-in-prod`. Open that file in Studio to see the rule and its reason.
</Aside>

## Changing the rules safely

- **Check before it applies.** Saving a file compiles the org's whole enforced set with it; a file that would break the set is refused, and nothing changes.
- **Shadow mode.** Switch a file to *shadow*: it is evaluated on real traffic and logs what it *would* have refused, without refusing anything.
- **Replay.** Before you switch a change on, replay it against the org's recent decisions to see which would change.
- **Four eyes.** With the org's *second approver* on, a change to policy files, an agent's own policy, retention or an agent's record waits until another admin approves it. Authors can't approve their own changes.
- **Broken files fail closed.** If a file that is in force stops compiling (for example after a project is renamed), what it guarded is closed and the console flags it, rather than silently opening up.

## Proving it later

- **Tamper evidence.** Each org's decisions form a hash chain whose heads are anchored hourly; [Verify](../../console/decision-log/) shows gaps or edits.
- **Retention and legal hold** per org; lifting a hold needs the second approver when four eyes is on.
- **Export** to a SIEM over OpenTelemetry logs, syslog or HTTP (Splunk HEC, Sentinel); an OpenTelemetry collector forwards to S3 or anywhere else.
- **Personal data** is redacted from the log's free text (emails, phone numbers, cards, IBANs, keys), and, for agents whose policy asks for it, from their transcripts and memory.
